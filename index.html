Words in Context

A concise, evidence-based guide to reading vocabulary through its surrounding text, with a lexicon and original practice questions.

1Overview

Words in Context questions ask you to select the word or phrase that best completes a short passage, or to determine the meaning of an underlined word as it is used. These items assess precision of meaning, not memorization: the correct answer is always the one the passage itself supports.

Two formats appear most often. In a completion item, a blank must be filled with the most logical word. In a meaning-in-context item, a familiar word is used in a less common sense, and you must infer which sense the author intends.

2Method

  1. Predict before you read the options. Cover the choices and supply your own word for the blank. Decide first whether its tone is positive, negative, or neutral.
  2. Locate the evidence. Find the clue in the passage: a definition, an example, a contrast, or a cause-and-effect relationship.
  3. Eliminate systematically. Remove options that conflict with the tone, the logic, or the evidence. Eliminating is often easier than selecting.
  4. Substitute and verify. Place the remaining option into the sentence and confirm that it preserves the author's meaning and reads naturally.

3Types of Context Clues

Clue typeSignal wordsExample and reading
Contrastbut, although, however, yet, despite, unlike"Although the critic was usually harsh, her review was ______." The blank opposes "harsh," so a word such as generous fits.
Continuationand, moreover, similarly, likewiseThe blank extends the preceding idea in the same direction and with the same tone.
Cause and effectbecause, thus, therefore, as a resultThe blank must be a logical reason for, or consequence of, the stated outcome.
Definition or examplecolon, dash, "such as," "that is"Material after a colon or dash usually explains the word directly. Read it as a definition.
Rule of thumb. A single transition word often decides the whole item. Identify whether the sentence changes direction or continues before considering any answer choice.

4Common Pitfalls

Choosing the impressive word

Advanced vocabulary is attractive, but an elegant word that does not fit the evidence is wrong.

Defaulting to the familiar sense

For polysemous words (bear, grasp, charge, subtle), the intended sense is dictated by the sentence, not by the first definition that comes to mind.

Ignoring tone

Two options may share a general meaning yet differ in connotation (frugal vs. stingy). Match the author's attitude.
